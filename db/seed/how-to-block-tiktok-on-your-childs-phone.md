If you're reading this, you've probably already tried asking. Maybe you've tried taking the phone. Maybe both, several times, and it ended the same way.

Here's the part nobody tells parents: **your child is not losing a discipline contest.** TikTok is engineered by thousands of people whose entire job is to keep them watching one more video. Expecting a fourteen-year-old to out-willpower that is like expecting them to out-argue a lawyer.

So the goal isn't to win the fight. It's to remove it.

## The fastest method (works today, both platforms)

You have three real options, and they are not equally good.

### 1. Built-in controls — free, but leaky

**iPhone (Screen Time):** Settings → Screen Time → App Limits → Social Networking → set a limit. You can also block outright via Content & Privacy Restrictions.

**Android (Family Link):** Install Family Link, link your child's account, then set a daily limit or block the app entirely.

**The catch:** both are well-known to teenagers. Screen Time passcodes get watched over shoulders. Family Link can be worked around by deleting and reinstalling in some setups, and older teens often know exactly how. Built-in tools are a reasonable starting point, not a solution.

### 2. Router-level blocking — strong at home, useless outside it

You can block TikTok's domains at your router. It works — until they leave the house or switch to mobile data. For a teen with a data plan, this buys you nothing.

### 3. A dedicated blocking app — the one that actually holds

A proper parental control app blocks at the device level, survives reinstalls, works on mobile data, and can't be quietly switched off without you knowing.

This is what we built **Parental Values** to do. When your child opens TikTok during blocked hours, they don't get a lecture — they get a calm screen explaining it's restricted, and something better to do instead.

## The part that makes it stick

Here's what separates families where this works from families where it becomes a nightly fight.

**Announce it before you install it.** A block that appears out of nowhere feels like an ambush, and ambushes get fought. Ten minutes of warning changes everything.

**Be specific about when, not whether.** "No TikTok" invites negotiation. "TikTok is off from 7 to 9 on school nights, and after 9:30 entirely" is a rule, and rules are easier to accept than moods.

**Say the real reason out loud.** Not "because it's bad." Try: *"I want you to have two hours a day that belong to you, not to an app."* Kids argue with authority. They argue much less with honesty.

**Give them a way to earn.** This is the biggest one. A block that only ever takes things away builds resentment. A block that can be *unlocked* by finishing homework, or completing something that takes a bit of brain, turns the whole thing from punishment into a trade.

## What to expect in week one

Be ready for this, because it's normal:

- **Days 1–2:** Genuine anger. Testing whether you'll cave.
- **Days 3–5:** Boredom, which parents often mistake for failure. It isn't. Boredom is where everything else starts.
- **Week 2:** Most families report the arguments drop sharply once the rule stops being renegotiated every night.

The thing that ruins it is inconsistency. One exception teaches them the rule is a negotiation, and you'll be having the same argument in a fortnight.

## The honest summary

| Method | Cost | Holds up? |
|---|---|---|
| iPhone Screen Time | Free | Weak — passcode gets learned |
| Android Family Link | Free | Moderate — workarounds exist |
| Router blocking | Free | Fails on mobile data |
| Dedicated app | Free / paid | Strong — device-level |

Start with the free tools if you want. Most parents end up somewhere sturdier within a month, usually after the third time they find the app reinstalled.

Whatever you choose, the block is the easy half. The conversation is what makes it last.
