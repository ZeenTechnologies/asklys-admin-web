Parents ask us this constantly, and the honest answer is more useful than the one they expect.

## The short version

There is no single number that works for every family. What matters far more than the total is **what the time replaces** — sleep, homework, conversation, or nothing much at all.

## What the research actually says

The average teenager spends 8 hours 39 minutes a day on entertainment screens, according to Common Sense Media — and that figure excludes schoolwork. For most families, the problem isn't the first two hours. It's hours six, seven and eight, which almost always come out of sleep.

## Four questions worth more than a timer

1. **Are they sleeping?** Screens after 10pm are the single strongest predictor of a difficult morning.
2. **Is schoolwork getting done?** Not perfectly — just getting done.
3. **Do they still have one thing they do offline?** Sport, music, drawing, anything.
4. **Can they stop without a fight?** This is the real test. A child who can put it down doesn't have a screen-time problem.

If the answer to all four is yes, the number on the timer matters much less than you think.

## What to do if the answer is no

Start with the boundary that costs the least conflict and gives the most back: **a hard stop at bedtime.** Not a reduction across the whole day — one clean line at night. It's the easiest rule to enforce and the one that changes the most.

From there, protect homework time. Then, if you need to, cap the rest.

> The families who succeed rarely start by cutting hours. They start by protecting sleep, then let everything else follow.

## Making the limit stick

A limit that depends on you policing it will fail, because you will get tired before your teenager does. The rule needs to enforce itself — that's what a schedule on the device does, and it's why "the phone locks at 9" works when "put your phone away" doesn't.

And give them a route back. A limit that can be extended by finishing something worthwhile stops feeling like a punishment and starts feeling like a deal — which is the difference between a rule that survives a month and one that doesn't.
